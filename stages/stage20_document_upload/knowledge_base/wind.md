# Wind Power

Wind power generates electricity by using moving air to spin large blades
connected to a turbine, which turns a generator. Most modern turbines are
horizontal-axis, three-bladed designs mounted on tall towers, since wind
speed and consistency increase with height above the ground.

Turbines are built with a cut-in speed (the minimum wind speed needed to
start generating, often around 3-4 meters per second) and a cut-out speed
(where the turbine shuts down to avoid damage in storms, often above 25
meters per second). Between those, output rises roughly with the cube of
wind speed, so small increases in wind speed produce large increases in
power.

Wind farms are built onshore, where land and grid access are cheaper, and
offshore, where winds are stronger and steadier but construction and
maintenance costs are much higher. A single utility-scale turbine typically
produces 2 to 5 megawatts.

The main advantage of wind is low cost per megawatt-hour in windy regions.
The main drawback, like solar, is intermittency — output depends on weather
and can vary hour to hour.
