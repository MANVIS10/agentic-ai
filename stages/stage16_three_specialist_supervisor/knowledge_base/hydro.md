# Hydropower

Hydropower generates electricity from the energy of moving or falling
water, most commonly by damming a river to create a reservoir and releasing
water through turbines at a lower elevation. The bigger the drop in height
(the "head") and the more water flowing through, the more power is
produced.

Large dam projects, like pumped-storage and reservoir hydro plants, can also
act as long-duration energy storage: during periods of low electricity
demand, extra power from the grid is used to pump water back uphill into
the reservoir, so it can be released again later when demand is high. This
makes hydropower one of the few renewable sources that can supply power on
demand rather than only when a resource (sun or wind) is available.

Smaller "run-of-river" hydro projects generate power directly from a
river's natural flow without a large reservoir, which has a much smaller
environmental footprint than a dam but produces less consistent output.

The main advantage of hydropower is that it is dispatchable and can store
energy at grid scale. The main drawbacks are the large upfront construction
cost and the environmental impact of damming rivers, which can disrupt fish
migration and downstream ecosystems.
